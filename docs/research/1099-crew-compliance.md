# 1099 crew: what the law actually says, and what workers fail to track

Research notes for the worker-education layer. Every claim below should be
re-verified against the cited primary source before it ships in-product.
State law varies; anything jurisdiction-specific is marked.

## The core distinction that everything hangs on

A 1099 contractor is not an employee. That single fact decides almost every
right a crew member thinks they have:

- No FLSA overtime, no meal/rest-break premiums, no waiting-time penalties,
  no minimum wage floor, no workers' comp presumption. Those attach to
  employment, not to a signed contract.
- The contractor's leverage is contract enforcement, accurate records,
  and tax position. Not labor law.

Sources: IRS worker-classification guidance; DOL Fact Sheet on
independent contractors vs. employees; state wage-theft statutes vary.

Important nuance for the product: misclassification cuts both ways. If a
"1099" gig looks like employment (set schedule, company tools, exclusive
work, no business license), the worker may actually be owed employee
protections and can file a complaint. Teaching workers to spot this is a
retention hook, not just compliance trivia.

## What workers should track and usually don't

Records to retain (IRS general rule: 3 years after filing; longer for
substantial omission, bad debt, property basis, or no return filed):

- Income records: invoices, payment confirmations, canceled checks, ACH
  records, 1099-NEC and 1099-K as received.
- Itemized receipts showing amount, time, place, business purpose.
- Contemporaneous mileage logs: date, destination, business purpose,
  miles. Vehicle deductions sit under IRC 274(d); reconstructed logs from
  memory are weak evidence. Commuting is not deductible.
- Timesheets, call/wrap times, deal memos, rate confirmations, production
  messages, approvals, payment terms. These are the dispute evidence when
  a production claims hours were different or pay was already sent.
- Contracts with kill-fee, weather-hold, and payment-term clauses, plus
  the signed deal memo itself.

## Tax items crew routinely miss

- Quarterly estimated taxes (federal and most states) and self-employment
  tax. Underpayment penalties apply even when the annual return is filed.
- State nonresident filings: working in a state you don't live in can
  create a filing obligation there even when your home state credits it.
- 1099 reconciliation before filing: match deposits against 1099-NEC,
  1099-K, and your own invoice records. Discrepancies are common and
  expensive to fix after the fact.
- Per diem under accountable-plan rules: GSA rates by locality, substantiation
  requirements, tax-home rules. Most gig apps ignore this entirely.
- Home office, equipment, kit rentals, union dues, agent fees, and
  reimbursed vs. non-reimbursed expense treatment on Schedule C.
- Mileage method choice: standard mileage rate vs. actual expenses have
  timing and switching constraints once elected.

## Threshold chaos worth explaining (verify numbers each tax year)

- Federal 1099-NEC reporting threshold and 1099-K thresholds have moved in
  different directions and states diverged. Most gig workers don't know
  the rules changed. A plain-language explainer has real search demand.
- Never promise a worker what their 1099s "should" total; forms lag and
  platforms report differently.

## Deal-memo clauses worth decoding for workers

- Payment terms: net-30 vs. net-60 vs. "upon receipt of funds" (which can
  mean never). Deposits and kill fees.
- Weather holds and force-majeure carve-outs: who eats the day?
- Overtime definitions in the memo vs. what the worker assumed.
- Liability/insurance requirements, COI demands on loan-out entities.
- Arbitration clauses and class-action waivers, where present.

## Where production companies get it wrong

Owners who come from the production side often don't track labor law
changes. Common exposure:

- Misclassifying workers who function as employees.
- State wage-theft notice requirements (e.g., CA WTPA, NY notices) that
  apply even to some contractor arrangements or are simply best practice.
- Unpaid-final-check rules and prompt-payment statutes that vary by state.
- Union contract obligations (IATSE local rules) they didn't read.

Flexzora should teach both sides. Companies that get this right are the
differentiated ones in a market full of producers who wing it.
